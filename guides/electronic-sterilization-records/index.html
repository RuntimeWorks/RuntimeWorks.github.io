Paper or electronic sterilization logs: what the rules say

Yes: you can keep your sterilizer logs on a screen, because none of the sources behind this page — the CDC texts, OSHA’s bloodborne pathogens standard, or the dental rules of New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Florida and Georgia — requires paper or sets rules for electronic signatures (US sterilizer rule pack, item K9).

What the rules require is the same in either medium: the operator’s name or initials and the monitoring results on every cycle’s entry (CDC/HICPAC, 2008 guideline for disinfection and sterilization), logs that are current (CDC, infection-prevention checklist for dental settings), and a retention period that follows your state (CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines); a good correction leaves the original entry visible.

Paper or electronic

The rules do not choose between paper and screens

None of the sources behind this page picks a medium for sterilizer logs. They set no format for these records and no requirements for electronic signatures (US sterilizer rule pack, item K9). The sources are silent on the medium and specific about the contents.

What to record

What every entry still needs

The medium is the one thing the sources leave open. The entry contents are the thing they spell out: for each sterilization cycle, record the type of sterilizer and cycle used; the load identification number; the load contents; the exposure parameters (e.g., time and temperature); the operator’s name or initials; and the results of mechanical, chemical, and biological monitoring (CDC/HICPAC, 2008 guideline for disinfection and sterilization).

  • Sterilizer and cycle usedWhich machine ran the load, and which of its cycles.
  • Load identification numberThe number that ties the packs on the shelf to this run.
  • Load contentsWhat went into the load.
  • Exposure parameters (e.g., time and temperature)What the machine showed during the cycle.
  • Operator’s name or initialsWho ran the load.
  • Results of mechanical, chemical and biological monitoringWhat each check showed.

For the log as a whole, the CDC checklist states the standard in one line: logs for each sterilizer cycle are current and include results from each load and comply with state and local regulations (CDC, infection-prevention checklist for dental settings).

On the operator field, the guideline asks for the name or initials and stops there (CDC/HICPAC, 2008 guideline for disinfection and sterilization). Nothing in the pack says an initial must be handwritten, witnessed or signed (US sterilizer rule pack, item K9).

The fields and what belongs in each one are covered in what a sterilizer log entry must contain.

Retention

How long to keep the records

This is where the sources do not agree, and this page does not pick a side for you. The CDC’s dental guidelines keep mechanical, chemical and biological monitoring records long enough to comply with state and local regulations (CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines). The 2008 guideline gives a length only as an example: a period that complies with standards (e.g., 3 years), statutes of limitations, and state and federal regulations (CDC/HICPAC, 2008 guideline for disinfection and sterilization).

Georgia is the only state in this pack with its own number in its rules: all sterilization records must be maintained for a period of not less than three (3) years (Georgia board of dentistry rule 150-8-.01). New York’s regulator states that there is no statute of limitations for charges of professional misconduct (New York State Education Department practice guidelines), and the pack summarises the whole position this way: only Georgia sets a minimum, one CDC text gives three years as an example, and one state points to an unlimited period for misconduct charges (US sterilizer rule pack, item K5).

Three years is a floor in one state and an example in one CDC text, not a national number (Georgia board of dentistry rule 150-8-.01; CDC/HICPAC, 2008 guideline for disinfection and sterilization). The state-by-state detail is in how long to keep sterilization records.

Corrections

Fixing a mistake without erasing the original

None of the sources says how a log entry must be corrected (US sterilizer rule pack, item K9). What a good log does anyway: the correction is added as a new line, the original stays visible, and the change shows when it was made and by whom. Nothing is erased or overwritten.

Dental Logbook will work that way: a correction supersedes the entry it fixes and keeps the original next to it, with the time of the change and who made it.

Scope

Where this page is silent

This page covers the CDC texts, OSHA’s bloodborne pathogens standard and the dental rules of five states: New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Florida and Georgia (US sterilizer rule pack, item K9). For every other state it is silent; check your state board’s current rules.

On the questions this pack does not answer — what makes an electronic signature valid, how a correction must be marked, what record-keeping software must do — the page stays silent instead of filling the gap with a guess.
