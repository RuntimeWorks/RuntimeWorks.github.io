Guides · Dental offices in the United States

Plain-language guides for dental sterilizer records

Guides on what to test, what to write down, how long to keep it, and what to do when a spore test fails. Each one starts with the answer, then shows where it comes from.

Testing

  • What is a spore test?

    A spore test, also called a biological indicator, is a preparation of bacterial spores that is put through a sterilizer cycle to check that the sterilizer works (CDC, 2008 disinfection and sterilization guideline).

  • How often must a dental office spore test its sterilizer?

    At least weekly for every sterilizer, using a biological indicator and a control from the same lot (CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines).

  • How to read and record a spore test result

    A spore test reads negative, positive or invalid, and the result counts only when the control grows.

  • The Bowie-Dick test: which dental sterilizers need it, and how often

    The Bowie-Dick air-removal test is for pre-vacuum steam sterilizers — the dynamic-air-removal kind (CDC, 2008 disinfection and sterilization guideline).

  • In-office or mail-in spore testing: how each works and what to record

    Both routes do the same job: a spore test, also called a biological indicator, is how the correct functioning of each sterilizer’s cycles should be verified, at least weekly and with a matching control from the same lot number (CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines).

  • Implant loads: the spore test, quarantine and what to do in an emergency

    Every sterilizer load that carries an implantable device gets a spore test, and the result is checked before the implant is used, whenever possible (CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines).

  • Chemical indicators: inside the pack, outside the pack, and what a failed one means

    Put a chemical indicator inside every instrument package, and add an external indicator on the outside whenever the internal one cannot be seen from the outside (CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines).

  • Does OSHA require spore testing in dental offices?

    No — OSHA’s bloodborne pathogens standard, 29 CFR 1910.1030, does not address sterilizer monitoring and says nothing about spore tests (OSHA, 29 CFR 1910.1030).

Records

  • What must a dental sterilizer log entry contain?

    A sterilizer log entry records what went through the machine, in which cycle, when, by whom, and what the monitors showed.

  • How long must a dental office keep sterilization records?

    There is no single federal number: the CDC tells dental offices to keep sterilization records — mechanical, chemical and biological — long enough to comply with state and local regulations (CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines).

  • How to label sterilized instrument packs in a dental office

    Write four things on every sterilized pack: the sterilizer used, the cycle or load number, the date of sterilization, and, if one applies, the expiration date (CDC, Summary of Infection Prevention Practices in Dental Settings, 2016).

  • OSHA bloodborne pathogens training records for dental offices

    Train every employee with occupational exposure at the time of initial assignment to tasks where that exposure may take place, and at least annually thereafter (OSHA bloodborne pathogens standard, 29 CFR 1910.1030).

  • CDC’s sterilization checklist items and the records that prove them

    CDC’s infection prevention checklist for dental settings lists five sterilization items in section II.6 — II.6.G, II.6.H, II.6.J, II.6.K and II.6.N — and this page quotes all five word for word (CDC, infection prevention checklist for dental settings).

  • How often dental staff need bloodborne pathogens training

    Train staff at the time of initial assignment to tasks where occupational exposure may take place, and at least annually thereafter, with the year’s session inside one year of the previous training (OSHA bloodborne pathogens standard, 29 CFR 1910.1030).

  • Infection control training and CE requirements by state

    Every dental office in the country trains each employee with occupational exposure at the time of initial assignment to tasks where that exposure may take place, and at least annually thereafter, with each year’s training within one year of the previous one (OSHA bloodborne pathogens standard, 29 CFR 1910.1030).

When something fails

  • What to do when a dental sterilizer fails a spore test

    When a spore test comes back positive, take the sterilizer out of service and repeat the test immediately, on the same cycle that produced the failure (CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines).
