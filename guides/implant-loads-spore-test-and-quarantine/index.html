Implant loads: the spore test, quarantine and what to do in an emergency

The short answer

Every sterilizer load that carries an implantable device gets a spore test, and the result is checked before the implant is used, whenever possible (CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines).

In an emergency, keeping implantable items in quarantine until the spore tests are known to be negative might be impossible (CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines).

Why implant loads are treated differently

Two schedules sit in one sentence: the weekly one, and the one that covers every implant load. CDC’s checklist puts them side by side — a biologic indicator, which is the spore test, is used at least weekly and with every load containing implantable items (CDC, Infection Prevention Checklist for Dental Settings). The 2003 dental guideline states the implant part on its own: use a biological indicator for every sterilizer load that contains an implantable device (CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines).

An implant goes into the patient and stays there. That is why these loads get more monitoring than a tray of instruments that will be reprocessed again tomorrow.

Quarantine: the implant waits for the result

The test only helps if somebody reads it before the implant is used. The 2003 guideline says the items are quarantined until the spore test results are known (CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines). The 2008 guideline says to use biologic indicators for every load containing implantable items and quarantine items, whenever possible, until the biologic indicator is negative (CDC/HICPAC, 2008 disinfection and sterilization guideline). CDC’s current dental page says that ideally, implantable items should not be used until they test negative (CDC, Best Practices for Sterilization Monitoring in Dental Settings).

Keep the hedges. The words in the sources are “whenever possible” and “Ideally”, not “never”, and this page does not round them up. Where the wording hedges, the hedge is part of the statement.

Quarantine here means what the word says: the implant is set aside, in its pack, while the test runs. It is a waiting state, not a judgement about the load.

In an emergency

Those hedges are there for a case, and the 2003 guideline names it: in an emergency, placing implantable items in quarantine until spore tests are known to be negative might be impossible (CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines).

So an implant can go out before its result. That is the exception and not the routine, and it does not end the process. If the test later turns positive, recall and quarantine any implantable items and do not use them until they are shown to be sterile (CDC, Best Practices for Sterilization Monitoring in Dental Settings).

The decision to let one go is made in the moment, by the people in the building. What lasts is the record of it, and that is the part worth getting right.

Dental Logbook takes the strict path by default. An implant load is held until its spore test reads negative. If an office lets one go early, the log asks why and keeps the reason with the load. If that test later turns positive, the log raises an alert with the load ID, so the office can go and find the patients — in its own patient charts. No patient names enter the logbook.

Never unwrapped

One sentence in the 2003 guideline carries no hedge at all: do not sterilize implantable devices unwrapped (CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines). There is no “whenever possible” in it.

What to record

A good log for an implant load answers four questions: what went through the cycle, which spore test rode with it, what the result was, and what happened to the implant.

  1. The load. The date, the sterilizer and the cycle type, the load number, and what was in the load.
  2. The implant flag. That the load was marked as containing an implantable device, and that a spore test went in with it.
  3. The spore test. Its product, lot and expiry; whether a control from the same lot went in with it, and what the control showed; whether it was read in the office or sent to a lab; and the result, with the date it was read.
  4. The implant’s status. Released after a negative result, or released before the result — and if so, the reason.
  5. The people. Who ran the cycle, and who entered the result.

No patient names go in this record. If an office has to find the patients who received an implant, the load number is the thread to pull, and the tracing happens in the office’s own patient charts.

Dental Logbook keeps these fields in one place, so an implant load can be read end to end without opening a second binder. Written down like this, the record answers the question an office actually gets asked months later: which implant, which load, which test, and what happened.
