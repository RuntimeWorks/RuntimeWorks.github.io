Dental sterilizer monitoring · United States

What is a spore test?

A spore test, also called a biological indicator, is a preparation of bacterial spores that is put through a sterilizer cycle to check that the sterilizer works (CDC, 2008 disinfection and sterilization guideline).

Dental offices run one on every sterilizer at least weekly, incubate it with a control from the same lot, and record the result (CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines).

What a spore test is

A biological indicator is an FDA-cleared commercial preparation of spores, for example Geobacillus stearothermophilus for steam, intended specifically for the type and cycle parameters of the sterilizer (CDC, 2008 disinfection and sterilization guideline).

In plain terms: bacterial spores are among the hardest things to kill. If a cycle kills the spores in the test piece, it killed the ordinary microbes on the instruments. The test checks the outcome of the cycle itself.

Why offices test at least weekly

Correct functioning of sterilization cycles should be verified for each sterilizer by the periodic use, at least weekly, of biological indicators (CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines).

CDC's short summary for dental settings says the same thing in one line: a spore test should be used at least weekly to monitor sterilizers (CDC, 2016 summary of infection prevention practices).

The sources quoted on this page say "at least weekly" and nothing about how to space the tests, and they set no separate schedule for a sterilizer that is used only part of the time. Schedules that go beyond this baseline are covered by the guides listed under Read next.

The control from the same lot

A spore test is run with a matching control: a biological indicator and a control from the same lot number (CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines).

The control is not processed through the sterilizer. It is incubated together with the test indicator (CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines).

In plain terms, the control answers one question: can the spores from this lot still grow? Without it, "no growth" could mean a good cycle or a test that was already dead. With it, the office knows the test was alive going in.

In the office or by mail

In-office biological monitoring is available, and mail-in sterilization monitoring services, for example from private companies or dental schools, can also be used to test both the BI and the control (CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines).

Both routes test the same pair, the test indicator and its control (CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines). What changes is where the spores grow and how the result travels back.

What changesIn the officeBy mail

What to record

The result of every spore test is written down, and sterilization monitoring records, mechanical, chemical and biological, are kept long enough to comply with state and local regulations (CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines).

A good spore test record holds the sterilizer and the cycle type; the load the test rode in; the indicator's product, lot number and expiration date; a note that the control came from the same lot and what it showed; how the test was run, in-office or by mail, with the dates; the result, negative, positive or invalid, and the date it was read; and who entered it.

How long to keep it? The sources quoted on this page give no number of years. They leave the length to state and local regulations (CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines).
