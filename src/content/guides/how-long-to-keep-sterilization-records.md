## What the CDC says

The CDC's dental guidelines leave the number to state and local rules. They say sterilization monitoring records — mechanical, chemical and biological — should be retained long enough to comply with state and local regulations <cite>(CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines)</cite>, and the same instruction appears in the CDC's summary for dental settings <cite>(CDC, 2016 summary of infection prevention practices)</cite> and on its current sterilization-monitoring page <cite>(CDC, 2024 sterilization-monitoring page for dental settings)</cite>.

The 2008 disinfection and sterilization guideline, which the CDC's dental pages point to, says to retain sterilization records for a period that complies with standards — three years is the example it gives — together with statutes of limitations and state and federal regulations <cite>(CDC/HICPAC, 2008 disinfection and sterilization guideline)</cite>.

OSHA sets no period for sterilizer logs. Its bloodborne pathogens standard keeps a different record set: training records must be maintained for three years <cite>(OSHA bloodborne pathogens standard, 29 CFR 1910.1030)</cite>.

## What each state says

Here is what each jurisdiction's own rules say about keeping sterilizer records.

Sterilizer records are not patient records. Patient charts carry their own periods: at least six years in New York <cite>(New York rule 29.2(a)(3))</cite>, five years in Pennsylvania <cite>(Pennsylvania rule 33.209(b))</cite> and ten years in Georgia <cite>(Georgia board of dentistry rule 150-8-.01)</cite>.

## What to record

A retention period only helps if the record itself is complete. For each sterilizer cycle, the CDC's 2008 guideline lists what to write down <cite>(CDC/HICPAC, 2008 disinfection and sterilization guideline)</cite>:

-   the type of sterilizer and the cycle used,
-   the load identification number and the load contents,
-   the exposure parameters, such as time and temperature,
-   the operator's name or initials, and
-   the results of mechanical, chemical and biological monitoring.

Mechanical monitoring means documenting that pressure, temperature and exposure time reached the levels the sterilizer manufacturer recommends, for every load <cite>(CDC, 2024 sterilization-monitoring page for dental settings)</cite>.

Packs are labelled with the sterilizer used, the load or cycle number and the date of sterilization, so that a failure can be traced to the packs it affects <cite>(CDC, 2016 summary of infection prevention practices)</cite>.

Logs for each sterilizer cycle are kept current and include the results from each load <cite>(CDC infection prevention checklist for dental settings)</cite>.

## How long to keep yours

Write the retention period into your infection-control policy instead of leaving it to habit. Where your state sets a minimum, that minimum is the floor: three years in Georgia <cite>(Georgia board of dentistry rule 150-8-.01)</cite>.

Where your state sets none, three years is the example the CDC gives, and the same guideline points to statutes of limitations as a reason to keep records longer <cite>(CDC/HICPAC, 2008 disinfection and sterilization guideline)</cite>.

New York's regulator states that there is no statute of limitations for professional misconduct charges, which is a reason to keep records longer than any minimum <cite>(New York State Education Department practice guidelines)</cite>.

A simple policy: keep each sterilizer record for at least three years, and keep any record that touches a failed load, a recall or an open question until that matter is closed.
