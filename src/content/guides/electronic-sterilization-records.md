## The rules do not choose between paper and screens

None of the sources behind this page picks a medium for sterilizer logs. They set no format for these records and no requirements for electronic signatures <cite>(US sterilizer rule pack, item K9)</cite>. The sources are silent on the medium and specific about the contents.

## What every entry still needs

The medium is the one thing the sources leave open. The entry contents are the thing they spell out: for each sterilization cycle, record the type of sterilizer and cycle used; the load identification number; the load contents; the exposure parameters (e.g., time and temperature); the operator’s name or initials; and the results of mechanical, chemical, and biological monitoring <cite>(CDC/HICPAC, 2008 guideline for disinfection and sterilization)</cite>.

-   **Sterilizer and cycle used**<br>Which machine ran the load, and which of its cycles.
-   **Load identification number**<br>The number that ties the packs on the shelf to this run.
-   **Load contents**<br>What went into the load.
-   **Exposure parameters (e.g., time and temperature)**<br>What the machine showed during the cycle.
-   **Operator’s name or initials**<br>Who ran the load.
-   **Results of mechanical, chemical and biological monitoring**<br>What each check showed.

For the log as a whole, the CDC checklist states the standard in one line: logs for each sterilizer cycle are current and include results from each load and comply with state and local regulations <cite>(CDC, infection-prevention checklist for dental settings)</cite>.

On the operator field, the guideline asks for the name or initials and stops there <cite>(CDC/HICPAC, 2008 guideline for disinfection and sterilization)</cite>. Nothing in the pack says an initial must be handwritten, witnessed or signed <cite>(US sterilizer rule pack, item K9)</cite>.

The fields and what belongs in each one are covered in [what a sterilizer log entry must contain](/guides/what-a-sterilizer-log-entry-must-contain/).

## How long to keep the records

This is where the sources do not agree, and this page does not pick a side for you. The CDC’s dental guidelines keep mechanical, chemical and biological monitoring records long enough to comply with state and local regulations <cite>(CDC, 2003 dental infection-control guidelines)</cite>. The 2008 guideline gives a length only as an example: a period that complies with standards (e.g., 3 years), statutes of limitations, and state and federal regulations <cite>(CDC/HICPAC, 2008 guideline for disinfection and sterilization)</cite>.

Georgia is the only state in this pack with its own number in its rules: all sterilization records must be maintained for a period of not less than three (3) years <cite>(Georgia board of dentistry rule 150-8-.01)</cite>. New York’s regulator states that there is no statute of limitations for charges of professional misconduct <cite>(New York State Education Department practice guidelines)</cite>, and the pack summarises the whole position this way: only Georgia sets a minimum, one CDC text gives three years as an example, and one state points to an unlimited period for misconduct charges <cite>(US sterilizer rule pack, item K5)</cite>.

**Three years is a floor in one state and an example in one CDC text, not a national number** <cite>(Georgia board of dentistry rule 150-8-.01; CDC/HICPAC, 2008 guideline for disinfection and sterilization)</cite>. The state-by-state detail is in [how long to keep sterilization records](/guides/how-long-to-keep-sterilization-records/).

## Fixing a mistake without erasing the original

None of the sources says how a log entry must be corrected <cite>(US sterilizer rule pack, item K9)</cite>. What a good log does anyway: the correction is added as a new line, the original stays visible, and the change shows when it was made and by whom. Nothing is erased or overwritten.

Dental Logbook will work that way: a correction supersedes the entry it fixes and keeps the original next to it, with the time of the change and who made it.

## Where this page is silent

This page covers the CDC texts, OSHA’s bloodborne pathogens standard and the dental rules of five states: New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Florida and Georgia <cite>(US sterilizer rule pack, item K9)</cite>. For every other state it is silent; check your state board’s current rules.

On the questions this pack does not answer — what makes an electronic signature valid, how a correction must be marked, what record-keeping software must do — the page stays silent instead of filling the gap with a guess.
